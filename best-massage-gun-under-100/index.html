What to Look for in a Massage Gun Under $100

Amplitude: The Most Important Spec

Amplitude (stroke length) determines how deep the percussion reaches into muscle tissue. The Theragun Pro ($299) has 16mm. Most sub-$100 guns have 10-12mm. For general soreness and daily recovery, 10mm is sufficient. For deep tissue work on dense muscles (quads, glutes, hamstrings), 12mm+ makes a noticeable difference. All five picks in this guide deliver at least 10mm — avoid guns that don't publish their amplitude, as they're typically 6-8mm (ineffective for anything beyond surface-level vibration).

Stall Force: How Hard You Can Press

Stall force is the pressure at which the motor stops. Budget guns stall at 20-30 lbs. The Renpho R3 handles 35 lbs; the Ekrin B37 handles 55 lbs. If you press hard into tight muscles (which you should — light pressure does nothing for deep tissue), low stall force makes the gun feel weak and stuttery. For personal recovery, 30-40 lbs is sufficient. For athletes and deep tissue work, 45+ lbs is necessary.

Noise Level: Quality of Life

The cheapest guns run at 60-70 dB — loud enough to be unpleasant during TV watching or conversation. Mid-range ($60-100) runs 45-55 dB. Theragun's QuietForce runs at 40 dB. If you use your massage gun while watching TV, near a sleeping partner, or on video calls, noise level is the difference between enjoyable and annoying. All picks in this guide run at 55 dB or below.

Battery Life and Charging

Most sub-$100 guns claim 4-8 hours of battery life, translating to 2-4 weeks of daily 10-minute sessions. Look for USB-C charging (Renpho R3, Ekrin) over proprietary barrel chargers (some budget models). Battery degradation after 18-24 months of daily use reduces runtime by 30-40%. The Ekrin B37's 8-hour battery and lifetime motor warranty make it the most durable pick.

Attachment Variety

Most guns include 4-6 attachments. You'll regularly use two: the large ball (large muscle groups) and flat head (dense muscles). The cone tip is for trigger points. The fork is for the spine and Achilles. Start with the ball head for everything; add others as needed. Don't choose a gun based on attachment count — a $39 gun with 8 attachments is still a $39 gun.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are cheap massage guns under $100 worth it?

Yes, for personal recovery. The gap between a $60 Renpho and a $299 Theragun Pro is real but smaller than marketing suggests. Budget guns deliver 10-12mm amplitude and 30-40 lb stall force, covering general soreness and post-workout recovery. The premium tier offers 16mm amplitude and 60 lb stall force, which matters for athletes but not casual users. For most people, a $59-89 massage gun provides 85% of the benefit at 20% of the cost.

What is amplitude in a massage gun?

Amplitude is the stroke length — how far the massage head travels into your muscle tissue. Higher amplitude means deeper penetration. The Theragun Pro has 16mm. Most sub-$100 guns have 10-12mm. For general soreness, 10mm is sufficient. For dense muscle groups (quads, glutes), 12mm+ makes a noticeable difference.

Can a massage gun replace a foam roller?

No, they complement each other. Foam rollers provide broad pressure across large muscle groups and connective tissue. Massage guns provide targeted percussion to specific trigger points. Use a foam roller for large-surface release and a massage gun for targeted knot work. Most athletes use both daily.

How often should I use a massage gun?

2-3 minutes per muscle group, 1-2 times daily. Avoid using on the same spot for more than 2 minutes to prevent tissue irritation. Always move the gun slowly across the muscle. Avoid joints, bones, the neck (carotid artery area), and the lower back during pregnancy.

Which massage gun attachments do I actually need?

You'll regularly use two: the large ball (large muscles) and the flat head (dense muscles). The cone tip is for trigger points. The fork is for the spine area. Most users ignore half the attachments — start with the ball head for everything and add others as needed.
